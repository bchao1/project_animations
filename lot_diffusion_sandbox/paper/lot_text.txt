ABSTRACT
Image and video diffusion models allocate equal computation to every region,
even when the intended scene calls for varying levels of detail. The spatial dis-
tribution of detail can often be anticipated before generation, indicating where
computation can be reduced. We introduce Level-of-Token (LoT) Diffusion, a
framework that turns this knowledge into an explicit multiresolution token lay-
out (Level-of-Token layout) for adaptive and efﬁcient generation. Tokens repre-
sent rectangular patches of varying sizes and shapes, allocating ﬁner tokens where
detail is needed and coarser tokens elsewhere. We adapt pretrained diffusion trans-
formers to LoT layouts through a patch-wise asymmetric ﬂow parametrization and
embeddings for multiresolution tokens, preserving full-resolution ﬂow prediction
at every denoising step while processing only a reduced token sequence. LoT Dif-
fusion enables layout-adaptive generation while preserving pretrained generative
priors. We demonstrate LoT with layouts derived from semantic masks, bounding
boxes, texture variance, and depth-of-ﬁeld cues, as well as agentic plans. Across
image and video generation, LoT offers favorable quality–efﬁciency tradeoffs,
with signiﬁcant speedups determined by the layout’s token budget.

INTRODUCTION
Image and video generative models produce remarkably high-quality content, yet generating high-
resolution images and long videos remains computationally expensive. Much of this cost comes
from diffusion transformers (Peebles & Xie, 2023) processing long sequences of spatial tokens
through attention and linear layers at every denoising step. Reducing this cost begins with rec-
ognizing that not all regions require the same level of detail: intricate textures, faces, and text re-
quire ﬁne detail, while clear skies, blurred backgrounds, and ﬂat surfaces can be synthesized more
coarsely. Pretrained models nevertheless allocate equal computation to all regions. Allocating re-
sources according to the detail needed is well established in level-of-detail rendering (Clark, 1976),
variable-rate shading (Vaidyanathan et al., 2014), and image compression (Shapiro, 1993). Whereas
rendering and compression operate on existing scene or image content, generation can begin with
a speciﬁed distribution of detail before the ﬁnal pixels are available. Notably, such knowledge can
come from the structure, description, or plan of the intended scene, which generative models in-
creasingly support through richer prompting and explicit layout speciﬁcation (OpenAI, 2026; Reve
AI, 2026; Ideogram, 2026a). These observations motivate using prior knowledge of where detail
matters to determine how to allocate compute for generation.
Ideally, a generative model would use this prior knowledge both to adapt where ﬁne detail is con-
centrated and to reduce computation. Spatial conditioning methods (Zhang et al., 2023; Mou et al.,
2024; Tan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Yang et al., 2023) guide generated content using spatial
inputs, but retain full-resolution processing and often introduce additional computational cost. Ef-
ﬁciency methods exploit redundancy through either reusing intermediate computations (Liu et al.,
2025a; Zou et al., 2024) or token reduction (Bolya & Hoffman, 2023; Lu et al., 2025; Xiao et al.,
2026; Jeong et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Chao et al., 2026), but do not utilize this
adaptivity as a control mechanism. Our goal is to use prior knowledge to control where ﬁne detail is
concentrated and allocate computation accordingly, enabling both adaptive and efﬁcient generation.
In this paper, we introduce Level-of-Token (LoT) Diffusion, in which an image or video is synthesized
from a content-adaptive, multiresolution token layout (Level-of-Token Layout). Each token repre-
sents a rectangular spatial patch with variable extent, speciﬁed by its height and width, allowing
important regions to be ﬁnely tokenized while less important ones are tokenized coarsely. Realizing
this on pretrained diffusion transformers requires adapting models trained on uniform grids to het-
erogeneous token layouts. To this end, we develop a formulation that preserves full-resolution ﬂow
prediction at every denoising step while the transformer processes only a reduced token sequence.
We achieve this through a patch-wise asymmetric ﬂow parameterization (Chen et al., 2026), together
with adaptations to token embeddings and positional encoding that account for various patch extents.
Fine-tuning pretrained models within our simple framework enables efﬁcient generation that adapts
to the speciﬁed layout while leveraging their generative priors.
We demonstrate LoT across diverse layout sources, including semantic masks, bounding boxes, tex-
ture variance, and depth-of-ﬁeld cues, as well as agentic plans. Generation speedup depends on the
token budget speciﬁed by the layout, reaching up to 2.04× for images and 3.53× for videos on av-
erage relative to the full-resolution pretrained models. Across the evaluated budgets, LoT offers fa-
vorable tradeoffs between generation quality and efﬁciency compared with existing token-reduction
methods. Qualitative results further show how generated content adapts to the speciﬁed token lay-
out. More results are in the Supplementary Website at https://iclr-submission7014.
github.io/supplementary/.
To summarize, our main contributions are:
• We introduce Level-of-Token Diffusion, a framework for efﬁcient image and video genera-
tion with token layouts that adaptively vary patch size and shape across an image or video.
• We adapt pretrained DiTs through patch-wise asymmetric ﬂow and embeddings for mul-
tiresolution tokens, enabling full-resolution ﬂow prediction at every step from a reduced
token sequence.
• We demonstrate favorable quality and efﬁciency tradeoffs across image and video genera-
tion, using layouts derived from scene cues and agentic plans.

RELATED WORK
Methods for efﬁcient generation.
A large body of work improves the efﬁciency of diffusion
models along several axes. Along the spatial axis, token merging and pruning techniques iden-
tify redundant tokens and merge or discard them based on predeﬁned heuristics or learned criteria
(Bolya & Hoffman, 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025; Haurum et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025;
Lee et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2025), while sparse attention mechanisms exploit sparse patterns in
the attention maps and use block-wise computation to reduce the quadratic cost of self-attention (Li
et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2025; Zhan et al., 2025; Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020; Choro-
manski et al., 2020; Beltagy et al., 2020). Along the timestep axis, feature caching methods reuse
intermediate features across denoising steps to avoid redundant computation (Liu et al., 2025a; Zou
et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b; Ma et al., 2024), whereas distillation and sampler-based approaches
reduce the number of denoising steps themselves, either by training a student to approximate a pre-
trained teacher (Yin et al., 2024b;a; 2025; Luo et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Salimans & Ho,
2022) or by deriving faster solvers and consistency-based samplers (Song et al., 2020; 2023). Other
progressive-upsampling methods grow the spatial resolution over the course of generation to save
computation in early stages (Jeong et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2026). However, these methods treat
spatial regions equally, operating along denoising-trajectory or attention-level axes independent of
how details are spatially distributed, and thus leave the prior knowledge of where content requires
computation unexploited.
Content-adaptive tokenization is an orthogonal and complementary axis that exploits exactly this
prior, accelerating generation by allocating compute based on the content. However, existing ap-
proaches address only part of the problem: some are spatially adaptive but with no or limited user
control (Chao et al., 2026; Haridas et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2025b; Zamﬁr et al., 2026; Zhao et al.,
2025a), others adapt the token budget without redistributing it across space (Yan et al., 2025; Bach-
mann et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2025; Duggal et al., 2024; Haji-Ali et al., 2026), and
still others achieve spatial adaptivity but require training from scratch and are incompatible with
pretrained diffusion models (Zhang et al., 2025c; Jia et al., 2025; Aasan et al., 2026). In contrast,
our method is spatially adaptive and compatible with various pretrained models, redistributing to-
kens non-uniformly according to an intended layout while additionally exposing user control over
the allocation. Most importantly, our method greatly accelerates image and video generation while
remaining compatible with the other efﬁciency techniques above.
Layout control for diffusion models.
A large body of work augments diffusion models with
explicit control over object placement and scene composition. Prior conditioning-based methods
attach trainable modules onto a frozen backbone, injecting spatial signals such as depth, segmenta-
tion masks, or bounding boxes, so that users can dictate where content appears (Zhang et al., 2023;
Mou et al., 2024). More recently, another line of work makes layout a direct input to the model
rather than a separate branch, encoding it into tokens that enter the denoising sequence (Tan et al.,
2025; Xiao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a) or expressing it as structured text in the prompt itself
(Yang et al., 2023). Most strikingly, the strongest deployed systems have made layout their primary
interface: Ideogram 4.0 is trained exclusively on structured JSON captions carrying a description
and a bounding box per element (Ideogram, 2026b), and Reve 2 derives a hierarchical layout from
the input text prompt before rendering from it (Reve AI, 2026). However, in all these methods the
layout merely decides what appears where, never how much compute is allocated to it. A region the
layout identiﬁes as empty sky is still generated on the same dense token grid as detailed typography.
In contrast, our method uses this prior knowledge of where detail matters to allocate computation
spatially, enabling content-adaptive generation with fewer tokens. Semantic masks and depth-of-
ﬁeld cues identify regions that can be generated more coarsely, allowing LoT to reduce computation
there while preserving ﬁner tokenization where detail is needed.

METHOD
Given a LoT layout deﬁned by P = {R1, . . . , RL}, our goal is to reduce the number of tokens
processed by the DiT while remaining compatible with pretrained models. Speciﬁcally, we use
patch-wise asymmetric ﬂow matching (Sec. 4.1) and introduce Level-of-Token DiT (Sec. 4.2) that
minimally modiﬁes the DiT architecture to handle LoT layouts. We present our method for image
generation, with a natural extension to video through per-frame LoT layouts. Fig. 3 shows our
overall pipeline.
4.1
FLOW MATCHING WITH LEVEL-OF-TOKENS
A LoT layout reduces the DiT sequence length from HW to L, but adapting a pretrained latent
diffusion model to this reduced sequence presents two challenges. First, the pretrained VAE decoder
requires a full-resolution latent grid of size H0 ×W0 ×D′. Second, the pretrained DiT predicts ﬂow
on the same sequence it receives, so directly applying it to the reduced sequence would only produce
L token-level ﬂow predictions, rather than the full-resolution ﬂow needed for denoising.

When ﬁtting Ae, we also estimate a scalar se by matching pro-

VAE decoding. Fitting and scaling details are provided in Sec. A.2 of the supplementary material.

RESULTS
Experimental Setup. For image generation, we ﬁne-tune FLUX.2-klein-base-4B (Black Forest Labs,
2025) on Aesthetic-Train-V2 (Zhang et al., 2025b) at 1024 × 1024, following (Chao et al., 2026),
with a random 95%/5% split for training and held-out evaluation. We also ﬁne-tune the 9B variant
using the separate training setup described in Sec. A.4. Image results use 4B unless otherwise noted.
For video generation, we ﬁne-tune Wan2.1-T2V-14B (Team Wan et al., 2025) on a selected 100k-
example subset of Vchitect-T2V-Dataverse (Fan et al., 2025), using 81-frame clips at 720P. We
reserve an additional 200 text-video pairs for held-out evaluation.
LoT-Flux supports extents (eh, ew) with eh, ew ∈{1, 2, 4, 8}, while LoT-Wan uses (1, eh, ew) with
eh, ew ∈{1, 2, 4}. Models used for layout-controlled generation are trained on mixed-source lay-
outs derived from semantic masks, object bounding boxes, reference-image texture variance, and
depth maps. We adapt the pretrained transformer projections with LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) and fully
ﬁne-tune the extent-dependent input and output heads and patch-size embeddings. See the supple-
mentary material for detailed training recipes: Sec. A.4 for images and Sec. A.10 for videos.

CONCLUSION
We introduced Level-of-Token Diffusion, a framework that uses level-of-detail information to guide
efﬁcient multiresolution image and video generation. By adapting pretrained diffusion models to
variable token layouts, LoT connects control over detail with computational savings. Across di-
verse layout sources, our results demonstrate generation that adapts to the speciﬁed layout, with
substantial speedups and favorable quality–efﬁciency tradeoffs over token-reduction baselines. We
note that scenes requiring ﬁne structures or textures throughout offer limited opportunities for token
reduction, and aggressive compression can degrade their ﬁdelity. LoT is therefore most advanta-
geous when detail requirements vary spatially, allowing computation to be reduced selectively. See
Sec. A.14 for a more detailed discussion.
Looking ahead, LoT suggests a closer connection between planning and efﬁcient generation. Our
demonstrations with agentic plans for images and videos provide an initial step, turning planned
spatial importance into an explicit computational allocation. Level-of-Token layouts could serve as
an editable intermediate representation, allowing multimodal systems and users to reﬁne a visual
plan and its computational budget. Extending this interaction to video could support faster iterations
between planning and synthesis in robotics and interactive simulation, where low generation latency
is essential for responsive interaction. More broadly, LoT offers a step toward generative systems
that use their understanding of the intended content to decide where computation is needed.

ETHICS STATEMENT
LoT builds on pretrained image and video generation models and inherits their underlying biases
and potential societal risks, including stereotypical representations and the generation of mislead-
ing or harmful content. Our changes to token allocation do not explicitly address these risks, and
ﬁne-tuning may alter the models’ behavior. Reducing the computational cost of generation can
broaden access to creative tools and research, but can also lower the cost of producing deceptive
media at scale. Potential misuse includes impersonation and non-consensual imagery, with associ-
ated privacy and intellectual-property concerns. Responsible deployment should therefore include
appropriate content safeguards, disclosure of synthetic content, respect for consent and applicable
usage restrictions, and application-speciﬁc bias and safety evaluation. Our quality and efﬁciency
evaluations do not establish the safety or fairness of downstream deployments.

We describe how we derive level-of-token layouts from semantic masks and bounding boxes, texture
variance (VRS), depth maps, and custom detail maps. The same spatial allocation rules apply to
images and video frames.
Semantic masks and bounding boxes.
During training, we sample up to three regions deﬁned
by semantic masks or bounding boxes. Masks can also come from S3OD (Kupyn et al., 2026),
using the same token-allocation procedure. Each selected region Mk is assigned a token level ℓk ∈

ℓbg and sample ℓk > ℓbg, so selected regions receive ﬁner tokens. The resulting spatial assignment

gives a detail ﬁeld D(q), where larger values request ﬁner tokens.

Training data and source mixture.

A.5
IMAGE GENERATION BASELINE COMPARISON DETAILS
Inference parameters.
For all baselines, we generate with 30 sampling steps, classiﬁer-free guid-
ance scale 4, and orthogonal guidance strength 1. We evaluate on all 5,261 prompts in the held-out
split. For each prompt, we derive the VRS layout from its corresponding held-out reference im-
age. This is a layout-conditioned comparison: the layout provides reference-derived spatial detail
allocation beyond the text prompt, but the reference RGB image is not an input to the generator.
Training Details.
For a fair comparison, we extend DDiT (Kim et al., 2026) and Foveated Diffu-
sion (Chao et al., 2026) to support token levels with spatial extents up to 8×8. For these comparisons,
we train ours, DDiT, and Foveated Diffusion for 20,000 steps each exclusively on VRS-derived grids
using the same settings: rank-256 LoRA with dropout 0.05 on attention, feed-forward, and timestep
projection layers, AdamW with learning rate 10−4 and weight decay 0.01, and a global batch size
of 16. We evaluate the 20,000-step power-function EMA checkpoint with γ = 7.
We also ﬁne-tune a full-resolution FLUX.2-klein-base-4B control on the same training split as our
VRS-only quantitative model, using the same LoRA target layers, rank 256, dropout 0.05, AdamW
learning rate 10−4, weight decay 0.01, global batch size 16, and 20,000 training steps.
Metric details.
We evaluate overall generation quality, content ﬁdelity, image quality, and efﬁ-
ciency. For overall generation quality, ImageReward (IR) (Xu et al., 2023), HPSv2.1 (Wu et al.,
2023), and HPSv3 (Ma et al., 2025) are learned predictors of human preference for prompt–image
pairs, capturing prompt alignment and visual appeal. Higher scores are better.
For content ﬁdelity, Fr´echet Inception Distance (FID) (Heusel et al., 2017) compares the feature
distributions of generated images and held-out reference images. Patch FID (pFID) applies the same
comparison to one native-resolution 299×299 center crop per image, emphasizing local appearance.
For image quality, TOPIQ (Chen et al., 2024) combines semantic information with local distortion
cues, while MUSIQ (Ke et al., 2021) assesses perceptual quality using multi-scale image represen-
tations. Both are used without a reference image or text prompt; higher scores are better.
For efﬁciency, runtime measures the CUDA-synchronized generation call, including prompt encod-
ing, diffusion sampling, and VAE decoding, on an H100 per image; it excludes grid preprocessing,
image saving, and model loading. We retain the original pretrained full-resolution FLUX.2 4B tim-
ing reference of 5.7783 seconds per image for the reported speedups. The ﬁne-tuned dense control
takes 5.7726 seconds per image (steady-state mean), corresponding to 1.001× speed relative to that
reference.

A.7
ADDITIONAL QUANTITATIVE COMPARISON: TOKEN BUDGET AND IMAGE QUALITY
We provide an additional comparison between LoT-FLUX.2-4B and pretrained FLUX.2 4B at
matched per-image token budgets, using all 5,261 held-out test prompts. We denote the pretrained
baseline as FLUX.2 (token matched): for each prompt, it uses a uniform square token grid whose
token count is as close as possible to that of our content-adaptive grid. It generates at the correspond-
ing lower resolution and is bicubically upsampled to 1024 × 1024 for evaluation. LoT-FLUX.2-4B
instead generates directly at 1024 × 1024 using spatially varying token extents.
Fig. 14(a) compares FID and pFID against the held-out reference images, together with the image-
quality metrics MUSIQ and TOPIQ, averaged over the test images at each threshold. Overall, LoT-
FLUX.2-4B achieves lower FID and pFID and higher image-quality scores than FLUX.2 (token
matched). This advantage is consistent with our adaptive allocation of ﬁner tokens to detailed re-
gions while retaining full-resolution output. In contrast, the pretrained baseline reduces resolution
uniformly, and subsequent upsampling cannot recover ﬁne details lost during low-resolution gener-
ation. Fig. 14(b) provides qualitative comparisons, with each token-matched output displayed at its
generation scale relative to our 1024 × 1024 output.
Figures 10–13 show how generation changes as the target token budget B varies for the same prompt.
We guide token allocation using semantic masks in Figures 10 and 11, and texture variance in
Figures 12 and 13. Lower budgets use larger tokens over more of the image, retaining recognizable
subjects and scene structure while simplifying ﬁne textures. Higher budgets support ﬁner spatial
detail, such as foliage, fur, and architectural features. These results illustrate a controllable trade-off
between computational budget and visual detail within the same model.

A.9
LAYOUT CONTROLLABILITY COMPARED WITH DENSE GENERATION
We evaluate whether changing the token layout changes where the generated subject appears, com-
paring the mixed-source LoT-FLUX.2-4B and 9B models with their corresponding pretrained full-
resolution FLUX.2 models.
Experimental setup.
We select 2,096 prompts from COCO-10k that describe one main subject
and have an unambiguous corresponding COCO bounding box. For each prompt, we construct two
layouts: one using the reference box and one randomly translating it while preserving its size. We
allocate 1×1 tokens inside the box and 2×2 tokens outside; the paired layouts have identical token
counts. Both model sizes use the same prompts, layouts, and seeds. LoT generates both images with
the same prompt and initial noise, changing only the layout. Dense FLUX.2 uses the same prompt
and initial noise but receives no layout; its single output is evaluated against both target regions.
This comparison measures the additional spatial adaptability afforded by the token layout, rather
than comparing two layout-conditioned models.
Metrics and results.
We use SAM3 (Carion et al., 2025) with the subject name, without providing
the target box, and retain the highest-conﬁdence subject mask above a conﬁdence threshold of 0.5.
Subject detection is the percentage of images with a detected subject. Target-region coverage is the
fraction of the detected subject mask lying inside the target box, |M ∩R|/|M|, with zero assigned
when no subject is detected. Tab. 4 shows higher coverage for LoT under both layouts, with overall
gains of 15.79 and 21.89 percentage points for 4B and 9B, respectively. Subject detection is slightly
lower for LoT at 4B and comparable at 9B. Thus, subjects more often occupy the ﬁne-token region
when generation can adapt to the supplied layout.
Figures 19 and 20 provide selected qualitative comparisons. With the prompt, initial noise, and
token count ﬁxed, relocating the ﬁne-token region changes the spatial placement of the subject in
LoT’s outputs; the dense result is shown alongside for reference.

A.10
VIDEO GENERATION TRAINING DETAILS
We ﬁne-tune Wan2.1-T2V-14B (Team Wan et al., 2025) with its native latent normalization and
frozen UMT5-XXL text encoder and VAE.
Training data and source mixture.
We use 81-frame clips from Vchitect-T2V-Dataverse (Fan
et al., 2025). The layout mixture comprises semantic masks or bounding boxes (50%, split equally),
VRS layouts (25%), and DoF layouts (25%). All training clips use 1280 × 720 resolution. Grids
retain et = 1 and eh, ew ∈{1, 2, 4}, with at most 3,072 tokens per latent time slice to avoid out-of-
memory errors.
Training setup.
We reuse the LoRA rank/dropout, AdamW coefﬁcients, adapter learning rate and
weight decay, clamped clean-data loss, and EMA schedule from Section A.4. Wan-speciﬁc LoRA
targets are the query/key/value/output projections in self- and cross-attention, the feed-forward lin-
ear layers, and the timestep embedding/projection layers. We fully ﬁne-tune the per-extent input
embeddings, output heads, and patch-size MLP at 10−5, and the ﬁnal output normalization and
modulation at 10−4. These fully ﬁne-tuned groups have zero weight decay. We train for 4,000 steps
with a total batch size of 32 videos, using FSDP2, gradient checkpointing, and gradient-norm clip-
ping at 200. Unlike the image model’s length-dependent dynamic shift, video training uses a ﬁxed
noise-schedule shift factor of 5, shared by all tokens within each clip.
A.11
VIDEO GENERATION BASELINE COMPARISON DETAILS
Inference parameters.
For all baselines, we generate with 50 sampling steps, classiﬁer-free guid-
ance scale 4, orthogonal guidance strength 1, and a noise-schedule shift factor of 5. We evaluate
on 200 held-out DataVerse test prompts, generating 81-frame videos at 1280 × 720 and 15 fps. We
use the test prompts to generate reference videos with MiniMax-H3 (MiniMax, 2026) and derive
the evaluation VRS layouts from these generated videos, rather than the original held-out clips. The
original clips were mostly static, so we use generated references to evaluate temporally varying token
allocations. Only the derived layouts, not the reference RGB videos, are supplied to the evaluated
models. At each token budget, ours, Foveated Diffusion, and ToMe-SD use the same VRS-derived
grids, prompts, and seeds.
Training Details.
We adapt Foveated Diffusion (Chao et al., 2026) to Wan2.1 14B and the same
VRS-derived token layouts as ours, with spatial extents up to 4 × 4. We evaluate our VRS-only
model and Foveated Diffusion at their 1,000-step checkpoints.
ToMe-SD (Bolya & Hoffman,
2023) requires no training: we adapt token merging to pretrained Wan2.1 14B by mean-merging
tokens within each VRS leaf for self-attention, cross-attention, and MLP computation, then copy-
unmerging the outputs before adding them to the full-resolution residual stream. Reported Full Res.
Wan2.1 14B is ﬁne-tuned using the same training budget and parameters on the same dataset for
fairness.
Metric details.
We evaluate six VBench (Huang et al., 2024) dimensions using its custom-video
evaluation on our held-out prompts. For image quality, aesthetic quality measures visual appeal,
while imaging quality assesses perceptual degradation such as blur and noise. For temporal consis-
tency, subject consistency measures the stability of subject appearance across frames, while back-
ground consistency measures the stability of the scene background. For motion, motion smoothness
measures the continuity of movement, while dynamic degree measures the prevalence of substantial
motion. Higher quality, consistency, and smoothness scores are better; a higher dynamic degree
indicates more motion, not necessarily more realistic motion.
For efﬁciency, runtime is the mean H100 generation time per video that includes both denoising and
VAE decoding, similar to the image generation case.

ADDITIONAL ABLATION STUDY RESULTS
Tab. 5 reports ablation results across different token budgets, extending the main-paper results in
Tab. 3, which reports only a single threshold setting at approximately 1.52× token compression.
We use the same training and evaluation setup as the image generation baseline comparison. We
introduce the ablation variants considered below.
Ours w/ direct HR prediction.
We bypass the analytic patch-wise asymmetric ﬂow recovery
in equation 9, introduced in Section 4.1, and directly interpret the per-extent output heads as the
full-resolution ﬂow prediction. The compressed input representation, transformer, and patch-size
conditioning are retained. The heads are trained against the full-resolution ﬂow objective without
the analytic recovery step.
Ours w/ binary grid.
We restrict the layout to square 1 × 1 and 2 × 2 tokens. This replaces the
multilevel, potentially anisotropic layout during both training and evaluation; patch-size condition-
ing and asymmetric ﬂow recovery remain unchanged.
Ours w/o size embedding.
We disable the learned patch-size embedding introduced in Sec-
tion 4.2, removing explicit extent-dependent conditioning of the transformer. We retain the extent-
speciﬁc input and output heads, single-scale RoPE at each token’s geometric center, and asymmetric
ﬂow recovery. This isolates the effect of explicitly informing the transformer of each token’s spatial
extent.

A.14
LIMITATIONS
LoT gains efﬁciency by allocating fewer tokens to regions that can be synthesized more coarsely.
When ﬁne structures or textures extend across most of an image, as in dense musical notation or
close-up views of grass or fabric, preserving them may require a ﬁne token layout throughout, leav-
ing limited room for computational savings. Enforcing a lower token budget in these cases can
degrade structural ﬁdelity. As shown in Fig. 22, the lower-budget result exhibits more fragmented
and distorted staff lines than the higher-budget result generated with the same prompt and noise
seed. Thus, the achievable savings depend on the scene’s detail requirements, and layout adaptation
does not guarantee faithful ﬁne structures under aggressive token reduction.
