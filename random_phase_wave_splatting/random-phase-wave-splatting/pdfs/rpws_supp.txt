Random-phase Wave Splatting of Translucent Primitives for
Computer-generated Holography — Supplementary Material
ANONYMOUS AUTHOR(S)
SUBMISSION ID: 1220
This document serves as the supplementary material to the paper “Random-
phase Wave Splatting of Translucent Primitives for Computer-generated Holog-
raphy”. The sections of this supplementary material are organized as follows.
In Section S1, we describe in detail how to splat a collection of random-
phase primitives using different alpha blending techniques to generated a
hologram. We also elaborate on the optimization of 2DGS models, triangle
splatting models, and textured meshes used for baseline comparisons and
the generation procedure of ground truth focal stacks and light fields used
for quantitative baseline comparisons.
In Section S2, we describe the experimental holographic display setup
and the complex wavefront phase-encoding schemes we used to validate
our algorithm.
In Section S3, we provide a detailed theoretical analysis based on statistical
optics that quantifies the various properties of holograms given their phase
characteristics, such as depth of field and bandwidth utilization.
In Section S4, we perform a theoretical analysis of our random phase alpha
blending algorithm. We derive exact convergence bounds of the cross term
in random-phase alpha blending and discuss optimal phase distributions for
alpha blending.
In Section S5, we provide a detailed theoretical analysis and mathematical
derivations of the proposed structured random phase algorithm that applies
random phase to primitives in a principled manner.
In Section S6, we provide extensive simulated experiments to validate our
method, including extended quantitative and qualitative baseline compar-
isons.
In Section S7, we show an extensive set of experimentally captured results.
S1
Additional Details on Software
In this section, we provide pseudocodes and implementation details
of the various algorithms and procedures described in the paper.
S1.1
Data Preprocessing
Random-phase Wave Splatting transforms a set of optimized 2D
primitives to holograms. We closely follow the holographics pipeline
described in Gaussian Wave Splatting (GWS) [3] to transform the
2D primitives in the world space to the SLM hologram space. Please
refer to GWS for more details.
S1.2
Implementations of CGH Algorithm Baselines
We provide the pseudocodes for the various baseline algorithms
described in our paper, including our Random-phase Wave Splatting
(RPWS) algorithm (Algorithm 3), Gaussian Wave Splatting (GWS)
proposed in [3] (Algorithm 1), and random-phase polygon-based
CGH using the silhouette method (Algorithm 2).
S1.3
Implementations of Alpha Blending Methods for
Wavefronts
We provide the pseudocodes for the various alpha blending and wave
compositing methods for random-phase primitives described in the
ablation study section in the main manuscript. Crucial modifications
are highlighted in magenta.
• Gaussian Wave Splatting (GWS) [3] (Algorithm 4): we naïvely
extend alpha wave blending to support random-phase prim-
itives by multiplying the primitive wavefronts by a random
phase map and then perform the GWS algorithm (Algorithm
1), which is the straightforward extension as described in
the GWS supplementary materials.
• GWS with square root alpha blending (Algorithm 5): we
integrate our square root alpha blending formulation with
GWS. Like RPWS, we optimize Gaussians to reconstruct the
intensity of the target image, and use the intensity-optimized
Gaussians for CGH.
• RPWS without square root alpha blending (Algorithm 6):
we remove the square root alpha blending formulation in
RPWS. Like GWS, we optimize Gaussians to reconstruct the
amplitude (original value) of the target image, and use the
amplitude-optimized Gaussians for CGH.
• Random-phase Wave Splatting (Algorithm 3): our final al-
gorithm that combines both back-to-front compositing and
square-root alpha blending.
S1.4
Details on 2DGS Models
We use the open source gsplat [30] library to optimize 2DGS mod-
els for Random-phase Wave Splatting CGH calculation. All models
are optimized using default parameters defined in the open source
code base.
To flexibly control the number of the optimized Gaussians, we
used the 3DGS-MCMC densification strategy [13] which is also sup-
ported in gsplat. All 2DGS models are constrained to a maximum
of 100k Gaussians throughout optimization.
For 2DGS models used for GWS [3], we optimized the Gaussians
to reconstruct the original target image (treating the target image
as the amplitude), and directly extract the per-Gaussian optimized
colors for GWS hologram generation. For 2DGS models used for our
RPWS algorithm, we optimized the Gaussians to reconstruct the
square of the target image (treating the squared target image as the
intensity), and take the square root of the per-Gaussian optimized
colors for RPWS hologram generation. This is necessary for our
square-root alpha blending algorithm, as described clearly in the
main paper and Sec. S4.
S1.5
Details on Textured Mesh Models
To be able to apply polygon-based CGH on meshes, the meshes are
required to have per-face colors since it is unclear how to apply tex-
ture mapping in polygon-based CGH. However, a disproportionate
number of triangles is required to reconstruct detailed appearance
when using per-face colors, therefore almost all high-quality mesh
models do not use per-face colors and leverage texture mapping.
Therefore, we start from optimized textured mesh models released

S1.6
Details on Triangle Splatting Models
We use the code provided by the authors of Triangle Splatting [10]
to optimize our soft-edged triangle splat models for RPWS. Similar
to 2DGS models for RPWS, we optimize triangle splats to reconstruct
the square of the target image, and extract the square root of the
per-triangle optimized colors for RPWS hologram generation.
S2
Additional Details on Hardware
S2.1
Experimental Setup
To experimentally validate our algorithms, we build a holographic
display benchtop prototype using a FISBA READYBeam™Ind2
fiber-coupled module with three optically aligned laser diodes with
wavelengths of 638, 521, and 443 nm, respectively, and a Holoeye
PLUTO phase-only liquid crystal on silicon (LCOS) SLM with a
resolution of 1920×1080 pixels, a pixel pitch of 8 𝜇m, and a precision
of 8 bits. We follow an optical configuration similar to those used
in Choi et al. [5] and Shi et al. [28], which is shown in Fig. S1. Color
images are captured with separate exposures for each wavelength
and combined in post-processing. All images are captured with a
FLIR Grasshopper3 2.3 MP color USB3 vision sensor. In our setup,
the target complex hologram is reconstructed at 4 cm away from
the SLM, and its depth range in hologram space is approximately 0.5
cm. With a 50 mm eyepiece, the Pluto SLM achieves a 4 mm eyebox
and 14◦horizontal FoV; simulated light fields use a 1.4 mm pupil.
We mount the camera sensor on a Thorlabs MTS25-Z8 25mm mo-
torized translation stage to experimentally capture 3D focal stacks
of the synthesized holograms. For horizontal parallax and light-field
acquisition, an adjustable pupil is positioned at the Fourier plane
and translated using a Newport CONEX-AGP motorized horizontal
stage.
S2.2
Phase Encoding
Our CGH algorithm encodes Gaussian-based scene representations
into a complex-valued wavefront, similar to other primitive-based
methods. While one can build a hardware setup with multiple SLMs
to modulate complex-valued wavefronts [26, 27], most holographic
display setups use phase-only SLMs, requiring phase encoding tech-
niques. To encode the complex-valued wavefront splatted onto the
SLM plane into a phase-only pattern, we use stochastic gradient
descent (SGD) as described in recent literature [4, 25] to synthesize
phase-only holograms that reconstruct a target complex wavefront.

S5.2
Phase Distribution of Primitives in the Spatial
Domain
In structured random phase, we applied random phase sampled
from the uniform distribution U(−𝜋, 𝜋) to the primitive wavefronts
in the Fourier domain instead of the spatial domain, i.e. applying
random phase to the spectrum of the primitive wavefronts. This
greatly differs from prior random phase CGH approaches where
random phase is simply applied in the spatial domain, and thus
the modulated wavefront can only achieve a flat angular emission
profile or fully diffuse reflectance properties.
It can be proved using the Central Limit Theorem that a uniform
phase distribution U(−𝜋, 𝜋) in the Fourier domain corresponds to
the same uniform phase distribution U(−𝜋, 𝜋) in the spatial domain.
This finding is crucial as it suggests by applying random phase to
the spectrum of the primitive wavefronts, the resulting hologram
still maximally utilizes the SLM bandwidth as the full 2𝜋modulation
range is used.

S6.5
Comparisons with STFT-based Light Field CGH
Despite belonging to fundamentally different algorithmic classes,
we delineate the key distinctions between RPWS and STFT-based
light field CGH methods. [4, 14]. STFT-based CGH methods require
densely sampled per-view light field target. To generate holograms
of a scene viewed from arbitrary viewpoints, the dense light field for
each viewing position is required, which involves re-synthesizing
the whole dense light field using either 3D modeling software or
novel-view synthesis algorithms. Due to such impracticality, all prior
STFT-based light field CGH works typically use synthetic datasets
and not real-world scenes. Furthermore, STFT-based methods re-
quire per-view iterative optimization, and suffer from spatio-angular
tradeoffs and poor generalizability in intermediate views due to the
window function used in STFT which we demonstrate int the quan-
titative comparisons below.
In contrast, our non-iterative method operates directly on pre-
optimized, off-the-shelf Gaussian splats, enabling arbitrary-view
rendering of real-world scenes without re-optimization of the 3D
representation for each individual view. To render holograms of dif-
ferent viewpoints of the same 3D scene, we simply transform each
primitive using the corresponding view transform of that view fol-
lowing the holographics pipeline described in GWS [3]. While we
do not claim superiority, RPWS addresses a fundamentally different
CGH regime that is optimized for real-world capture and forward
compatibility with state-of-the-art primitives-based 3D representa-
tions, benefiting from any ongoing advances in geometric primitive
splatting.
It is important to note that the high image quality reported by
prior STFT-based methods is largely a consequence of the high-
quality synthetic datasets they rely on, rather than the inherent
superiority of the algorithm. In real captured 3D scenes, these meth-
ods must also depend on novel view synthesis of primitive-based
scene representations (e.g., Gaussians, triangles). Consequently, they
would suffer from the exact same artifacts that are identical to those
observed in RPWS, which arise from the limitations of the underly-
ing 3D representation, not the CGH algorithm itself. Any attribution
of these artifacts to RPWS itself is therefore a misinterpretation. As
primitives-based 3D reconstruction methods continue to improve,
these artifacts are expected to diminish, eventually allowing the
underlying representation to achieve near-perfect image quality,
just like synthetic datasets.

S7.1
Experimentally Captured Baseline Comparisons on
Focal Stacks
We show experimentally-captured 3D focal stacks and zoomed-in
patches of holograms synthesized using different CGH baselines
and phase-encoded using SGD, including our Random-phase Wave
Splatting (RPWS) algorithm with Gaussians [12] and triangle splats
[10], random-phase polygon-based CGH (Polygons-RP) using the
silhouette method [18, 19, 21] and their time-multiplexed variants
(1 frame, 8 frames, and 24 frames), and Gaussian Wave Splatting
(GWS) [3] in Figs. S26 to S32.
The experimentally captured focal stacks well match the simula-
tion results. RPWS achieves the best balance between high in-focus
image quality and natural defocus blur. GWS achieves good in-focus
image quality but suffers from ringing coherent blur artifacts and
limited blur size in defocus regions. The in-focus image quality of
polygon-based CGH is inherently limited by the per-face colored
mesh 3D representation. With additional time-multiplexing, image
quality significantly improves for all random-phase CGH methods.
S7.2
Experimentally Captured Baseline Comparisons on
Light Fields
We show experimentally-captured horizontal light fields and zoomed-
in patches of holograms synthesized using different CGH baselines
and phase-encoded using SGD, including our Random-phase Wave
Splatting (RPWS) algorithm with Gaussians [12] and triangle splats
[10], random-phase polygon-based CGH (Polygons-RP) using the
silhouette method [18, 19, 21] and their time-multiplexed variants
(1 frame, 8 frames, and 24 frames), and Gaussian Wave Splatting
(GWS) [3] in Figs. S33 to S38.
The experimentally captured horizontal light fields and paral-
lax results closely match our simulations. The GWS baseline ex-
hibits little to no parallax across views due to its limited effective
eyebox. Polygon-based CGH produces larger parallax but suffers
from degraded image quality caused by the coarse, textured mesh
representation. In contrast, RPWS achieves both high image qual-
ity and greater parallax than GWS. Furthermore, applying time-
multiplexing substantially enhances image quality for all random-
phase CGH methods.
Additioanlly, we capture the horizontal parallax of RPWS holo-
grams at multiple focal depths, as shown in Fig. S39 and Fig. S40.
The observed view disparities scale proportionally with depth differ-
ences. Nearby objects exhibit larger shifts when focused at distant
planes, while distant objects shift more when focused near. These
results confirm that the reconstructed parallax is geometrically con-
sistent across all depth planes.
S7.3
Discussions on Contrast
Our RPWS algorithm generates random-phase holograms. However,
despite achieving similar image quality in simulation, random-phase
holograms often experimentally exhibit significantly lower contrast
than smooth-phase holograms. This discrepancy stems primarily
from the fringing field effect (pixel crosstalk) inherent in liquid
crystal-based SLMs [23]. Random-phase patterns are characterized
by high-frequency spatial features with abrupt phase discontinuities
between adjacent pixels. The SLM’s inability to physically resolve
these sharp transitions leads to modulation errors that degrade
diffraction efficiency and increase background noise, resulting in
reduced contrast.
Learning-based calibration is a potential solution for improving
random-phase hologram contrast [4–6, 9, 11, 16, 25]. These methods
train a neural network on datasets of SLM phase inputs and cap-
tured outputs, creating a proxy forward operator that accounts for
hardware imperfections. This learned model then replaces the ideal
angular spectrum method used in hologram optimization to close
the simulation-to-reality gap. Specifically, Kuo et al. [16] successfully
modeled pixel crosstalk by introducing a learned blur kernel into
the forward operator. These techniques are critical for maximixing
the contrast of random-phase holograms.
