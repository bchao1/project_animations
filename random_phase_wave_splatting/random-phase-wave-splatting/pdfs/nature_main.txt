Random-phase Wave Splatting of Translucent
Primitives for Computer-generated Holography
Brian Chao1,†, Jacqueline Yang1,†, Suyeon Choi1,2,
Manu Gopakumar1, Ryota Koiso1,3, and Gordon Wetzstein1,∗

Holographic near-eye displays offer compact, eyeglasses-like form factors for virtual and
augmented reality, but require computer-generated holography (CGH) algorithms that
convert expressive 3D scene representations into physically faithful holograms. Here we
introduce Random-phase Wave Splatting (RPWS), a new primitive-based CGH formula-
tion for composing large collections of continuously translucent random-phase wavefronts
into holograms with correct color, occlusion, defocus, and parallax. The central challenge
is that independently phase-modulated primitives do not obey conventional alpha com-
positing: interference cross terms alter the reconstructed intensity and require a differ-
ent compositing law. We derive this law in the intensity domain and show that translu-
cent random-phase wavefronts alpha blend linearly in expectation, leading naturally to
square-root wave compositing and establishing time multiplexing as the statistically exact
mechanism for realizing alpha blending of random-phase primitives. Because the RPWS
formulation depends on phase statistics and continuous alpha rather than a particular
primitive geometry, it applies to arbitrary translucent primitives, including Gaussians
and soft-edged triangles. Simulations and experiments validate accurate color and occlu-
sion together with large eyebox, natural defocus blur, parallax, and high-quality 3D focal
stack and 4D light ﬁeld reconstruction.

wavefront that is propagated to the SLM. Yet modern neural scene representations increasingly
rely on translucent primitives with continuous opacity, including Gaussians22,23 and soft-edged

ing across overlapping primitives, rather than binary visibility alone. Translating this rendering
paradigm from ray-based graphics to coherent wave optics therefore raises a fundamental ques-
tion for CGH: how should the complex wavefronts generated by large collections of translucent
primitives be physically composited into a single hologram while retaining the appearance and
visibility encoded by the original scene representation?
The difﬁculty becomes acute when the primitive wavefronts carry random phase. Although
phase is not directly observable, its distribution determines the spatio-angular behavior of the
reconstructed light ﬁeld.27–30 Smooth-phase holograms31 can achieve high in-focus image qual-
ity,1,2,32,33 but concentrate energy in the low frequencies of the angular spectrum, producing
a restricted eyebox, limited depth-dependent defocus, and increased sensitivity to pupil posi-
tion.29,34 Random-phase holograms are well established in CGH and distribute energy over a
substantially larger angular range, enabling wide parallax and natural defocus blur;34 their rapid
phase variations, however, create interference and speckle that are commonly mitigated through
partial coherence or time multiplexing.19,35–40 Existing primitive-based CGH methods do not
provide a general solution for combining this random-phase behavior with continuously translu-
cent scene primitives. Polygon-based CGH can assign random phase to mesh surfaces,41–47 but
generally relies on opaque triangles and silhouette-based occlusion, preventing the continuous
alpha blending central to modern splatting representations. This makes ﬁne scene appearance
dependent on dense polygonal geometry and per-face texturing, rather than allowing multiple
soft primitives to overlap and jointly explain image content. GWS, conversely, efﬁciently alpha
blends millions of translucent Gaussian wavefronts, but its formulation assumes a near-constant
or smooth phase across primitive surfaces.1 GWS brieﬂy postulates a heuristic for introducing
random phase to an individual Gaussian primitive, but does not derive a random-phase wave-
splatting formulation or a mechanism for compositing large numbers of overlapping translucent
random-phase primitives. Applying this heuristic independently to every primitive while re-
taining the same amplitude-domain blending rule does not produce valid random-phase wave
splatting: once independently phased wavefronts overlap, interference cross terms alter the re-
sulting intensity and break the correspondence to conventional alpha compositing. The resulting
hologram exhibits incorrect color and occlusion together with erroneous defocus and parallax,
as we demonstrate in Table 1, Fig. 1, and throughout the paper. Thus, while random-phase
holography itself is well established, a principled wave-splatting framework for compositing
thousands or millions of translucent random-phase primitives has remained unresolved.
Here we derive the compositing principle required for Random-phase Wave Splatting
(RPWS). The key distinction is that smooth-phase and random-phase primitive wavefronts
obey fundamentally different alpha-blending rules. For smooth-phase primitives whose ﬁelds
add coherently, conventional alpha blending can be formulated in the amplitude domain, as
in GWS.1 For independently randomized primitive wavefronts, however, the interference cross
terms are nonzero for any single phase realization and conventional amplitude-domain blending
fails. We show that these cross terms vanish statistically over independent random-phase real-
izations and that the desired alpha composite is instead recovered in expectation in the intensity
domain. Matching this expected intensity to the standard ray-based alpha-compositing equation
directly yields a square-root alpha-blending rule for the constituent wave amplitudes and makes

Building on this principle, RPWS establishes random-phase wave splatting as a new primitive-
based CGH formulation for compositing continuously translucent random-phase wavefronts.
RPWS converts 3D representations composed of translucent 2D primitives into time-multiplexed
random-phase holograms through an intensity-domain square-root alpha rule and back-to-front
wave propagation, allowing large collections of continuously translucent primitives to be com-
bined without assuming matched or smooth phase. Because the compositing rule depends on
phase statistics and continuous alpha rather than a particular primitive geometry, RPWS de-
ﬁnes a general random-phase wave-splatting framework for a broad class of translucent scene
representations. Existing primitive-based approaches address only subsets of this problem:
polygon CGH can incorporate random phase but generally assumes opaque geometry, whereas
prior wave-splatting methods support continuous alpha under smooth-phase assumptions. We
demonstrate RPWS with both 2D Gaussians22,23 and soft-edged triangles,24–26 showing that the
same compositing formulation operates across distinct primitive types. The resulting holograms
preserve accurate color and occlusion while utilizing substantially more of the SLM space–
bandwidth product, enabling a larger eyebox, wide parallax, and natural depth-dependent de-
focus. Through simulated and experimentally captured 3D focal stacks and 4D light ﬁelds, we
show that RPWS reconstructs sharp in-focus content together with perceptually correct defo-
cus and view-dependent appearance, while time multiplexing progressively suppresses residual
interference and converges toward the statistically correct alpha-blended reconstruction. These
results establish random-phase wave splatting as a general CGH framework for directly trans-
forming emerging translucent primitive-based 3D scene representations into holograms with the
spatial and angular ﬁdelity required for next-generation near-eye displays.

Translucent primitive representations. Since the advent of 3DGS,22 signiﬁcant research has
focused on developing alternative geometric primitives for 3D scene reconstruction. Among
these emerging primitive-based representations, 2D translucent primitives are notable for their
local manifold structures, which enable explicit normal computation and surface extraction.
Their ﬂat-surface formulation integrates naturally with rasterization pipelines and aligns well
with silhouette-based methods for polygonal CGH, allowing us to draw inspiration from prior
work.8,11,41,47

Random-phase wave splatting. We develop Random-phase Wave Splatting (RPWS), a CGH
framework for compositing random-phase translucent primitives with continuous alpha values
into a single holographic wavefront. In RPWS, each primitive wavefront is effectively modu-
lated by a random phase map φi(x) to distribute the light passing through each primitive away
from the optical axis. The primitive wavefronts are then composited from back to front with
respect to the SLM using a wave-propagation rule coupled to intensity-domain alpha blending,
and multiple independently sampled random-phase realizations are time multiplexed to realize
the desired intensity distribution. This formulation makes random-phase wave splatting possi-
ble without assuming that overlapping primitive wavefronts share a matched or smooth phase,
while preserving continuous opacity, color, and occlusion across large collections of primitives.

Random-phase Alpha Blending
RPWS departs fundamentally from GWS1 and prior silhouette-based methods47 in its treatment
of alpha blending. While earlier smooth-phase CGH approaches assumed amplitude-domain
alpha compositing, we show that for random-phase wavefronts alpha blending is linear in the
intensity domain in expectation. This crucial distinction, which is largely overlooked in prior
work and GWS, directly leads to our wave compositing equation (Eq. 5), where square-root
terms and time-multiplexing naturally emerge, in contrast to GWS. We analyze different alpha
blending formulations in detail in Fig. 3 and further below in this section.

Evident from Eq. 14, we emphasize that time multiplexing is not merely an optional add-on
for speckle reduction, but a fundamental requirement for correct alpha blending in our frame-
work. The alpha blending equation is inherently stochastic: it is only satisﬁed in expectation
over random phases, and any single realization introduces cross-term interference that corrupts
the blend. Time multiplexing ensures that these cross terms vanish statistically, thereby real-
izing the mathematically correct alpha blending behavior in practice. In this sense, speckle
reduction is only a by-product; the primary role of time multiplexing is to enforce the valid-
ity of the probabilistic alpha blending formulation itself. We further derive exact convergence
bounds in the supplemental materials, showing that the uniform 2π phase distribution belongs to

Ablation study on random-phase wave compositing. To our knowledge, no effective method

operations required by RPWS—intensity-domain square-root alpha blending and back-to-front

ing the depth-discontinuity and occlusion-border artifacts produced when either component is
omitted.

Simulation and Experimental Results
We generate 3D holograms of scenes from the Blender59 and MipNeRF-36060 datasets using
2D Gaussians, textured meshes, and translucent triangles, and build a benchtop holographic

Baseline comparisons with simulation results. We compare RPWS against several primitive-
based CGH baselines, including random-phase polygon-based CGH (Polygons-RP) via the sil-
houette method11,42,43 and its time-multiplexed variants (1 and 8 frames), as well as Gaussian

RPWS to 2DGS representations (RPWS-GS), where the number of Gaussians can be matched
directly to the number of polygon primitives (using 3DGS-MCMC61) for fair comparisons, sim-
ilar to GWS. For RPWS with soft-edged triangle primitives,24–26 which we denote as RPWS-∆,
we pick triangle splatting26 as the representative method. Since triangle splatting does not allow
for exact primitive count control, we only show qualitative results of RPWS-∆.
Fig. 4-(a) and 5-(a) compare simulated focal stacks of the synthesized holograms. GWS
produces smooth-phase holograms with little depth-dependent variation, yielding unnatural co-
herent blur with ringing artifacts. Single-frame random-phase holograms (Polygon-RP, RPWS)
exhibit strong speckle, but quality improves with time multiplexing (8, 24 frames). Polygon-RP
produces more natural defocus blur, but its in-focus ﬁdelity is limited by the low-quality per-

defocus blur, closely matching incoherent blur, while maintaining in-focus quality comparable
to GWS.

Discussion
Random-phase Wave Splatting establishes a new primitive-based CGH regime for wave-optical
rendering of translucent random-phase primitives. By deriving the intensity-domain composit-
ing rule required for independently phase-modulated primitive wavefronts, RPWS preserves
the continuous alpha relationships of modern splatting representations while distributing optical
energy across the available SLM bandwidth. This combination enables accurate color and oc-
clusion together with natural depth-dependent defocus, wide parallax, and a larger eyebox. The
formulation is not speciﬁc to Gaussian geometry: we demonstrate the same wave-compositing
principle with Gaussian and soft-edged triangle primitives, suggesting a direct path for con-
verting emerging translucent primitive-based scene representations into holograms without re-
designing the compositing model for each representation. In this sense, RPWS extends wave
splatting from the smooth-phase regime to a distinct random-phase regime whose image forma-
tion is governed by intensity-domain statistics and time multiplexing.
That said, in RPWS, time multiplexing is used to realize mathematically exact alpha blend-
ing, hence many frames are required for accurate reconstruction. In practice, the effectiveness of
time multiplexing is constrained by SLM refresh rates, though this limitation may be alleviated
by advances in high-speed SLMs, such as the 3600 fps FLCoS used in Holographic Parallax34
and the recently developed 5760 fps TI MEMS-based PLM; addressing quantization artifacts in
these devices presents an interesting future direction. Our experiments also show reduced con-
trast due to random phase, but extending learning-based calibration techniques19,33 to directly
reconstruct random-phase complex ﬁelds4 may further enhance RPWS holograms. Please refer
to the supplemental materials for more discussions on contrast enhancement strategies.
Methods
Data preparation and hologram synthesis
We generate 3D holograms of scenes from the Blender59 and MipNeRF-36060 datasets using 2D
Gaussians, textured meshes, and translucent triangles. Following GWS,1 we prepare 3D scene
representations with the open-source gsplat library49 for 2DGS, NeRF2Mesh models62 for
textured meshes, and Triangle Splatting models26 for translucent triangles. Additional data
preparation details are provided in the supplemental materials.
We implement our algorithms in PyTorch and build a benchtop holographic display for
experimental validation. Please refer to the supplementary materials for detailed pseudocode
and hardware speciﬁcations.
Statistics and reproducibility
This work is a methods and algorithms contribution; no statistical hypothesis testing was per-
formed. Quantitative results (Table 2) report standard deterministic image-quality metrics (PSNR,
