target images. Our implementation uses a target binary pattern consisting of 18 × 11 white dots
where every dot is spaced 70 pixels apart. Therefore, the region of interest (ROI) has 700 × 1190
pixels and the projection area is around 12.4 × 21.7 cm2.
D. Laser Power Calibration
In order to incorporate the light efficiency loss function in our optimization process, it is important
that we know the maximum intensity sum ∑|usrc|2 that could possibly be recorded by the camera
sensor. To measure this, we display a zero-phase pattern on the SLM and capture the output
intensity on the display screen. A constant zero-phase pattern indicates no phase modulation of
the collimated laser beam at the SLM plane, and thus the reflected wavefront should simply be a
plane wave. We perform this calibration to compute ∑|usrc|2 for all three color channels.

We implement all optimization algorithms and model training procedures in PyTorch, and we
describe our implementation in detail in the following sections.

operator fp could either be the angular spectrum method fASM [4] or a learned propagation

For the learned wave propagation model, we use the same architecture as in Choi et al. [5]. Since
we are only working with 2D projection, we train our model on one fixed propagation z, which
is the distance from the SLM to the 2D hologram plane. As shown in [6], the distance from
the SLM to the hologram plane needs to be calibrated per color channel, and the final distance
measurements are 19.7, 19.9, and 20.1 cm for red, green, and blue channels, respectively. We use a
batch size of 4 and a learning rate of 4e−4. We generate 4800 phases using SGD-ASM for each
color channel and captured the intensities on the projection screen to generate a paired dataset.
Instead of capturing images formed on the hologram plane, we capture the images displayed
on the projection screen since this would allow the model to additionally learn to compensate
for the existing artifacts induced by the projection screen. The dataset is partitioned into train,
validation, and training sets with a ratio of 8:1:1. The training takes around 24 hours to converge
on an NVIDIA A6000 GPU.

5. THE EFFECT OF PROPAGATION DISTANCE ON IMAGE QUALITY
Fig. S7. Comparison of experimentally captured images optimized using different propagation
distances. Visible wave-like artifacts are shown in the zoom-in crops of the long propagation
distance (z = 0.64 d∗) case.
To perform light reallocation effectively, the propagation distance z from the SLM plane to the 2D
hologram plane needs to be long enough such that the sub-hologram cones of the SLM pixels
cover the whole projection region. However, it is generally hard to produce good image quality
using a long propagation distance due to tricky issues in frequency domain sampling. There has
been extensive research on more clever wave propagation algorithms that improve holographic
image quality [8, 9].
Here, we show SGD with camera-in-the-loop (CITL) [1] optimization results using different
propagation distances. As shown in Fig. S7, long propagation distances give rise to severe fringe
artifacts and chromatic blur, suggesting that naive SGD might not work with long propagation
distances. Hence, we leverage a learned wave propagation model to compensate for these
artifacts.
6. THE EFFECT OF LIGHT EFFICIENCY LOSS FUNCTION WEIGHT
Fig. S8. Qualitative and quantitative results of varying the light efficiency loss function regular-
ization weight. By increasing the regularization weight, we are able to constrain more light to
the projection region.
We show experimentally captured 2D hologram results optimized using different light efficiency
loss function weights in Fig. S8. As the loss function weight increases, we observe that more
light is restricted within the projection region, improving image brightness. However, the image
quality slightly drops. Thus, the light efficiency loss function weight is a parameter that should

7. EXPERIMENTS ON ASM PROPAGATION AND CAMERA-IN-THE-LOOP (CITL) OPTI-
MIZATION
Other than SGD-model presented in the main paper, we also show images optimized using an
ideal wave propagation model (SGD-ASM) and camera-in-the-loop (CITL) techniques [1] (SGD-
CITL) in Fig. S9 and compare with SGD-model. For the ideal wave propagator, we use the angular
spectrum method [4]. Our light efficiency loss function works with both cases although less
significant, demonstrating the importance of using a camera-calibrated learned wave propagation
model. Similar to SGD-model, SGD-CITL corrects the illumination nonuniformity artifacts present
in SGD-ASM but generates fringe artifacts. We suspect that this is mainly due to the incorrect
gradients that arise from long propagation distances accumulating over CITL optimization steps.

9. THE EFFECT OF ROI SIZE ON IMAGE BRIGHTNESS AND IMAGE RECONSTRUC-
TION QUALITY
In our main setup, the native resolution of the SLM is 800 × 1280 pixels and the resolution of
the region of interest (ROI) on the SLM is 700 × 1190 pixels. This translates to a large projection
region of 12 × 21 cm2 after the 2D hologram is magnified onto the projection screen. SLM pixels
outside of the ROI are not optimized.
The reason we used a ROI slightly smaller than the SLM resolution is illustrated in Fig. S11. It
is impractical to set the ROI to the full support of the wavefield or a spatial extent much larger
than the SLM area because such a setting would lead to bad image quality, as shown in Fig.
S11. The degrees of freedom of the SLM pixels are not enough to generate high-image quality
holograms with spatial resolution significantly larger than the native SLM resolution. As the ROI
decreases, we see that the image reconstruction performance gradually improves. This is why
most prior works in CGH [1, 2, 5–7] have an ROI slightly smaller than or equal to the native SLM
resolution. Our goal is to improve the brightness of the system while maintaining image quality
for a comfortable viewing experience. Hence, we follow prior works and also employ the setting
where the ROI is slightly smaller than the SLM resolution.
Fig. S11. Here, we optimize SLM phases to reconstruct a 2D sinusoidal pattern, as shown in
Fig. S12. Optimizing a 2D hologram whose spatial resolution is larger than the SLM resolution
leads to bad image reconstruction quality. As the ROI decreases (leftward on the horizontal
axis), the image reconstruction quality greatly improves.
Fig. S12 shows the relationship between brightness improvement and ROI size. In our projector,
the ROI size is close to the SLM size (1.0 in the plot), and we see a decent improvement in
brightness. In other applications where we only need a small projection region, i.e. the ROI is
small, we observe an even more drastic improvement in image brightness and light efficiency
when the 2D holograms are optimized using our loss function. From the small ROI optimization
results in Fig. S12(b), we see that most light is not redirected to the ROI when our loss function is
not applied. The plot in Fig. S12(a) demonstrates that the brightness of a 2D sinusoidal intensity
pattern improves by 10-fold for a small ROI size of 100 × 160 pixels when images are optimized
with the light efficiency loss function. This suggests that our loss function is very crucial for small
projection region applications or cases when the SLM resolution is much higher than the required
projection region resolution.

10. BRIGHTNESS IMPROVEMENT COMPARISON BETWEEN SIMULATION AND EX-
PERIMENTAL RESULTS
We average the brightness improvement of holograms when using our loss function over the
whole testing set. In simulation, the brightness improvement is 66%. For the experimentally
captured results, the brightness improvement is 33%. Although this seems like a fairly large
difference, we believe this discrepancy can be attributed to the the way we performed our
brightness calculation based on the scale factor s (see Section 4 of supplementary materials). The
scale factor s is computed such that the reconstructed hologram best matches the target hologram
intensity in terms of pixel-wise mean-squared error. This makes the brightness calculation very
sensitive to the captured image quality. In our experimental setup, there are multiple sources of
error during capture, such as camera dark shot noise, which effects the scale factor s and hence the
brightness calculation. In the future, we plan to use more precise methods to measure the total
luminous flux of the projected holograms and compute actual brightness of the image.
Another source of error comes from the total energy term in Eq. 3 in the manuscript. In practice,
we calculate the total allocatable energy by calculating the intensity sum of the captured image
when a blank phase pattern is displayed on the SLM. However, errors such as camera dark shot
noise will affect the precision of measurement. In the future, we plan to use more precise methods
to measure the total luminous flux of the projected holograms and compute the actual allocatable
energy of the holograms.
In conclusion, although there is a mismatch between the simulated and captured hologram
brightness improvement, we believe our brightness calculation method serves as a good proxy
and that more precise measurement methods can be adopted in the future to bridge the gap
between experimentally captured and simulated results.
